# Ad copy

The words of an ad are the script, the lyrics and the closing line. The look
decides how the ad is built; this decides what it says.

Use the user's words as given. A script, a line or a name the user wrote stays
exactly as written unless they ask for changes.

Find the one thing the buyer wants and say it plainly. Not the feature, not
"quality": the moment it changes. "Ice still in your bottle at 4pm", not
"double-wall insulation". Write to the person who buys, not the person who uses
it (the parent, not the baby). Relief from a real annoyance usually moves faster
than a big promise.

The hook is the first seconds. One short line that opens a question the rest of
the ad answers, said over a picture that makes the same point with the sound
off. Keep the product out of the first frame unless it is the surprise.

Never invent numbers, awards, reviews, results or deadlines. Use the facts the
user or their Brand gives; when a claim has no fact behind it, leave it out.

Write for the ear:
- Short sentences, contractions, plain words, one idea per line, about 3 spoken
  words a second.
- Numbers and units as spoken words in voice lines: "four p.m.", "thirty days",
  "nineteen ninety-nine".
- No em dashes in spoken lines; a voice can't say them. Use "and", "but", "so",
  or end the sentence.
- Every line is something a camera could film. A line you can't picture becomes
  generic footage.
- Cut the words every ad uses: unlock, elevate, seamless, game-changer,
  next-level, "it's not just X, it's Y".

The closing line names the product and the one thing to do next, using the
user's real offer when there is one: "Get the commuter bottle today."

Examples:
- Hook: "Your coffee's cold by ten. Mine isn't."
- Spoken line: "Ice still clinking at four p.m., so the afternoon slump gets a
  cold drink, not a warm one."
- Lyric: "Ice at noon, ice at four / Grab your bottle, out the door."
- Closing line: "Cold till you're done. Order today."
